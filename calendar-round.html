Fifty-two years, then it all repeats

Run the Tzolk'in and the Haab' side by side and a full Tzolk'in+Haab' pairing — like 4 Ajaw 8 Kumk'u — returns only after the least common multiple of 260 and 365: 18,980 days, or exactly 52 Haab' years and 73 Tzolk'in rounds.

The mechanism

Two gears

The left wheel is the 260-day Tzolk'in; the right is the 365-day Haab'. The red mark on each begins at the mesh point. Step the day count forward and the marks separate — they line up together again only after 18,980 days.

—
A counting fact

Why only 18,980 combinations

There are 260 × 365 = 94,900 ways to pair a Tzolk'in day with a Haab' position on paper. Only 18,980 of them ever actually occur.

The reason: 365 mod 20 = 5, so as the Haab' advances one full year the Tzolk'in day sign advances 5 positions. A given day sign can therefore only ever fall on Haab' day-numbers that differ by multiples of 5 — never on an arbitrary one. Every real Calendar Round date threads this constraint.

The 52-year period mattered

Completing a Calendar Round was a genuine threshold. It is best documented for the Aztec/Mexica as the xiuhmolpilli ("binding of the years") and its New Fire ceremony; the Maya equivalent is less theatrical in the record but the 52-year bundle is a real structural unit, and Maya art shows year-bundle iconography.

The catch

A Calendar Round date is ambiguous

Because the pairing repeats every 52 years, "4 Ajaw 8 Kumk'u" alone cannot say which 4 Ajaw 8 Kumk'u — the one in 3114 BCE, or 52 years later, or any of dozens since. For events inside a single lifetime that is usually enough. For history across centuries it is not.

This is exactly the problem the Long Count solves: an absolute day tally from a fixed era-base that never repeats. Classic monuments typically give both — the Long Count to fix the date, the Calendar Round to name its day.
