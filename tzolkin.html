Tzolk'in · 13 × 20

The 260-day sacred calendar

Two independent gears — the numbers 1–13 and a fixed ring of 20 named days — turn together. A given number returns to a given day only after 260 steps (13 and 20 share no common factor). This count is attested across Mesoamerica for well over two millennia and is still kept today by K'iche', Ixil, Mam, Q'anjob'al and other daykeepers.

Why 260?

Origin theories

No source states why the count is 260 days. Several explanations are argued; they are not mutually exclusive, and none is proven.

  • Human gestation. ~260 days approximates the interval from a missed first menses to birth; midwife-daykeepers in living tradition make this link explicitly.
  • Zenith passage. Near 14–15° N (the latitude of Copán and much of the southern lowlands) the interval between the sun's two annual zenith transits is close to 260 days on one side of the year.
  • Agricultural cycle. 260 days is roughly the maize planting-to-harvest span in parts of the highlands.
  • Ritual arithmetic. 13 (levels of the upper world / a sacred number) times 20 (a full count, and the number of fingers and toes — the vigesimal base).
  • Venus and eclipses. 260 divides evenly into longer astronomical periods used in the codical tables (e.g. 2 × 260 = 520 ≈ 3 eclipse half-years).

Antiquity

The earliest securely dated 260-count references come from Oaxaca (Monte Albán, San José Mogote Monument 3, ~600 BCE) — that is, from Zapotec rather than Maya contexts. The system was pan-Mesoamerican; the Maya inherited and elaborated it rather than inventing it.
